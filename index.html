Abstract

We present and analyse an interactive implementation of the constant-factor polynomial-time approximation algorithm of Ashur, Filtser, Katz & Saban (2022) for vertex-guarding a weakly visible polygon (WV-polygon). Starting from Bhattacharya et al.'s boundary guard construction and the terrain-like PTAS of Mustafa & Ray, the algorithm first approximates a minimum boundary guard set for \(\partial P\), then adds a bounded number of additional guards to cover every interior hole, yielding a \((2+\varepsilon)\)-approximation of the global optimum. All algorithms are implemented in TypeScript with live interactive demonstrations throughout.

1 Introduction

The concept of guarding a polygon is a highly studied aspect of computational geometry, particularly in association with the Art Gallery problem. To guard a polygon is to place points, called guards, such that every point of the polygon is visible from at least one guard. A point is visible from another if the line segment connecting them lies fully within the polygon.

There exist many variants of this problem. The general Art Gallery problem has been proven to be \(\exists\mathbb{R}\)-complete [1], meaning any problem over real-number values can be reduced to it. Guarding a simple polygon with guards restricted to polygon vertices is NP-hard [2] and even APX-hard [3]: any NP problem admitting a constant-factor PTAS reduces to this problem.

Ghosh presented an \(O(\log n)\)-approximation for vertex-guarding a simple polygon [4], and conjectured the existence of a polynomial-time constant-factor approximation. Bhattacharya et al. later proposed a 6-approximation that turned out to be incomplete: their algorithm guaranteed (up to approximation) the guarding of the polygon's boundary, but not its interior [5].

This project demonstrates the constant-factor polynomial-time approximation algorithm discovered by Ashur, Filtser, Katz & Saban for vertex-guarding a weakly visible polygon (WV-polygon), confirming Ghosh's conjecture for this class [6]. The algorithm works in two stages:

  1. Compute a \((1+\varepsilon)\)-approximate vertex guard of the boundary \(\partial P\).
  2. Add at most \(|G|\) additional guards to cover every interior hole.

The result is a \((2+2\varepsilon)\)-approximation of the optimal guard set. In the following sections we characterise WV-polygons, show how to guard \(\partial P\), and then guard the polygon's interior.

2 Description

2.1 Weakly visible polygons

A polygon \(P\) is said to be weakly visible from an edge \(uv\) if for every point \(p \in P\), there exists a point \(g \in uv\) that sees \(p\). To detect this property we use:

Proposition 1 [7, 8]
Let \(uv\) be an edge of polygon \(P\), with \(u\) to the right of \(v\). Then \(P\) is weakly visible from \(uv\) if and only if
  • the shortest path from \(u\) to any vertex \(w \in P\) only ever takes left turns, and
  • the shortest path from \(v\) to any vertex \(w \in P\) only ever takes right turns.

To test this, we compute the Shortest Path Tree (SPT) of the polygon. Writing \(\pi(u, x)\) for the shortest Euclidean path from \(u\) to \(x\) within \(P\), the SPT from vertex \(u\) is \[ \SPT_P(u) \;=\; \bigcup_{v \in V(P)} \pi(u, v), \] a tree whose vertices are those of \(P\) and whose edges form the shortest paths from \(u\) to all other vertices.

To compute \(\SPT_P(s)\) from a vertex \(s\), we first triangulate \(P\) and form the dual graph \(D\). Iterating over \(D\) from the triangle containing \(s\), each step crosses a diagonal \(uv\) and admits an unprocessed vertex \(w\). Let \(a\) be the farthest common ancestor of \(u\) and \(v\) in \(\SPT_P(s)\). Then \(\pi(a,u)\) and \(\pi(a,v)\) form a funnel \(F(uav)\) — the two paths diverge from \(a\) [8].

For each diagonal \(uv\) with unprocessed vertex \(w\), we find the tangent \(x\) to the funnel \(F(uav)\). Assuming \(x \in \pi(a,u)\), we split \(F(uav)\) into \(F(uxw)\) and \(F(wau)\), adding edge \(xw\) to \(\pi(s,x)\) and gradually building \(\SPT_P(s)\).

To test weak visibility of \(P\) from \(uv\) we:

  1. Compute \(\SPT_P(u)\) and \(\SPT_P(v)\).
  2. Verify that all paths in \(\SPT_P(u)\) turn left, and all paths in \(\SPT_P(v)\) turn right.

Theoretically this takes \(O(n)\) using Chazelle's triangulation [9] and finger trees [8]. Our implementation uses ear-clipping triangulation and plain JS arrays, running in \(O(n^3)\).

2.2 Guarding polygon boundaries

With a way to detect WV-polygons in hand, the next step is to approximate a minimum vertex guard for the boundary \(\partial P\) of a WV-polygon \(P\). We adapt the work of Ashur et al. [6].

2.2.1 Constructing a witness set

Denote \(\partial P\) the boundary of polygon \(P\). We define a witness set \(W\) of points along \(\partial P\) such that if a vertex set \(G\) guards \(W\), it also guards all of \(\partial P\).

For each vertex \(v\) of \(P\), compute the visible sections of \(\partial P\) from \(v\). The endpoints of all these sections split \(\partial P\) into \(O(n^2)\) pieces. Taking one point from every piece gives \(W\):

Proof
Assume \(G\) guards \(W\). Let \(p_i \in W\) with \(p_i \in P_i\), and let \(g \in G\) see \(p_i\). Then \(p_i\) lies in a visible section from \(g\); since \(p_i \in P_i\), \(g\) sees all of \(P_i\). As every \(p_i\) is guarded, every piece \(P_i\) is guarded, hence \(\partial P\) is guarded. \(\square\)

2.2.2 A PTAS for guarding the witness set

Before describing the algorithm, two observations are needed:

Observation 1
Finding a minimum vertex guard \(G \subset V\) for \(W\) is equivalent to finding the minimum dominating set of the visibility graph of \(G \cup W\). Indeed, every \(w \in W\) must be adjacent to some vertex in \(G\) in the visibility graph — this is exactly the guard condition.
Observation 2
The visibility graph of boundary points of a WV-polygon is terrain-like: if vertices \(a, b, c, d \in V\) appear in counter-clockwise order, and \(a\) sees \(c\) and \(b\) sees \(d\), then \(a\) must see \(d\). Failing this would create a boundary crossing that contradicts weak visibility. 1

The terrain-like property lets us apply the PTAS of Mustafa & Ray [10], which yields a \((1+\varepsilon)\)-approximation for any minimum hitting set satisfying a locality condition. Applied here, the algorithm is:

Local-search boundary guard
Given \(\varepsilon > 0\), set \(k = c^2\varepsilon^{-2}\) for a suitable constant \(c\).
  1. Start with an arbitrary dominating set \(G \subseteq V\) for \(W\).
  2. If any \(k\) guards of \(G\) can be replaced by \(k-1\) guards from \(V \setminus G\) while still dominating \(W\), perform the swap and repeat.
  3. Stop when no improving swap exists.

This returns a dominating set of size at most \((1+\varepsilon)\cdot\OPT\).

2.2.3 Proving the PTAS

The locality condition of Mustafa & Ray [10] requires that for any disjoint \(R, B \subseteq \mathcal{P}\) in range space \((\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{D})\), we can build a planar bipartite graph \(H\) between \(R\) and \(B\) such that for every range \(D \in \mathcal{D}\) with \(R \cap D \neq \emptyset\) and \(B \cap D \neq \emptyset\), there exist \(r \in R \cap D\), \(b \in B \cap D\) with \((r,b) \in H\).

For our setting this translates to: for every vertex \(v\), there exist \(r \in R\) and \(b \in B\) both dominating \(v\) with \((r,b)\) an edge of \(H\). The planarity of \(H\) is established using the terrain-like property; the full proof is given in [6].

2.2.4 Summary

In summary:

  1. Compute the witness set \(W\) of \(P\).
  2. Apply the local-search algorithm above to obtain a \((1+\varepsilon)\)-approximate guard set for \(\partial P\).

Our implementation uses \(k = 2\): we prune individually redundant guards (\(k=1\) swaps) and perform 2-for-1 guard swaps (\(k=2\) swaps). Assuming \(\OPT > c^2 = 8\), this gives \(\varepsilon = 2\), so at most a 3-approximate boundary guard.

The naive implementation runs in \(O(n^{2k+1}) = O(n^5)\) in the worst case. In practice, greedy initialisation makes the local search converge in very few iterations.

2.3 Guarding holes

A guard set for \(\partial P\) does not automatically guard the interior of \(P\). Interior holes — maximal convex connected subregions not seen by any guard — can still exist. We need to identify and cover them.

The following sections are inspired by and closely paraphrase Ashur et al. [6]. Any interpretation not in the original is marked with \((\star)\).

2.3.1 Properties of constructed edges

A constructed edge formed by guard \(v\) is an interior chord of \(P\) on the boundary of \(\Vis(v)\). Every constructed edge \(xy\) is adjacent to a pocket \(C_{xy} \subset P\) invisible from \(v\).

  • \(xy\) is an upper edge if the pocket \(C_{xy}\) is below the line \(\ell_{xy}\) through \(x\) and \(y\).
  • \(xy\) is a lower edge if \(C_{xy}\) is above \(\ell_{xy}\).

We write \(\partial C_{xy}\) for the section of \(\partial P\) covered by the pocket.

Claim 1 \((\star)\) — Boundary leaning
Let \(xy\) and \(x'y'\) be two constructed edges such that \(C_{xy}\) and \(y'\) lie on the same side of \(xy\). Then \(y' \in \partial C_{xy}\).
Proof. Let \(w = xy \cap x'y'\). Since \(y'\) and \(C_{xy}\) are on the same side and \(w \in xy\) sees \(y'\), we have \(y' \in C_{xy}\). As \(y' \in \partial P\) it follows that \(y' \in \partial C_{xy}\). \(\square\)
Claim 2 \((\star)\) — Lifted pockets
If \(x \in uv\), then \(C_{xy}\) is weakly above \(x\). If \(x \notin uv\), then \(C_{xy}\) is strictly above \(x\).
Proof. If \(x \in uv\): \(C_{xy} \subseteq P\) and \(P\) is weakly above \(uv \ni x\), so \(C_{xy}\) is weakly above \(x\). If \(x \notin uv\): \(C_{xy} \cap uv = \emptyset\) (otherwise \(uv \subset C_{xy}\) contradicting weak visibility). Every \(z \in C_{xy}\) has a sightline to some \(z' \in uv\), and since \(z' \notin C_{xy}\) the chord \(zz'\) must cross \(xy\), forcing \(z\) strictly above \(xy\) and hence above its lower endpoint \(x\). \(\square\)

2.3.2 Properties of holes

A hole \(H\) in \(P\) with respect to guard set \(G\) is a maximal convex connected subregion not visible from any guard. Holes are convex because they are intersections of half-planes (the non-visible side of each constructed edge).

Claim 3 \((\star)\) — Downward hole boundary curving
Let \(H = (h_1, \ldots, h_k)\). If edge \(h_1 h_2\) lies along an upper edge \(xy\) with \(x\) below \(y\) and \(x \notin uv\), then \(h_2 h_3\) lies along another upper edge \(x'y'\) with \(h_2\) lower than \(h_3\).
Proof. Since \(x \notin uv\), Claim 2 gives \(H \subset C_{xy}\) strictly above \(x\). By Claim 1, \(y' \in \partial C_{xy}\), so \(y'\) is strictly above \(x\). Similarly \(x \in \partial C_{x'y'}\), and by Claim 2 \(x\) is weakly above \(x'\). If \(y'\) were below \(x'\), then \(x\) would be above \(y'\) — but \(x\) is strictly below \(y' \in C_{xy}\), a contradiction. Hence \(y'\) is above \(x'\) and \(h_2\) is lower than \(h_3\). \(\square\)
Claim 4 \((\star)\) — \(uv\)-touching
Every hole \(H = (h_1,\ldots,h_k)\) has an edge along an upper edge whose lower endpoint lies in \(uv\).
Proof. \(H\) touches at least one upper edge. If none of these has a lower endpoint on \(uv\), Claim 3 forces the boundary of \(H\) to curve upward indefinitely — but \(H\) is convex and finite, so eventually there must be a downward-curving edge. Downward curvature is only possible when the lower endpoint reaches \(uv\) (otherwise Claim 3 forbids it). Contradiction. \(\square\)
Claim 5 \((\star)\) — Subsequent \(uv\)-touching
If \(h_1 h_2\) lies along an upper edge \(xy\) with \(x \in uv\) below \(y\), then the adjacent edge \(h_2 h_3\) lies along an upper edge \(x'y'\) also with \(x' \in uv\).
Proof. Since \(xy\) and \(x'y'\) intersect at \(h_2\), and \(h_1 \in C_{x'y'}\) is on the same side as \(x\), Claim 1 gives \(x \in \partial C_{x'y'}\), so \(x\) is weakly above \(x'\) by Claim 2. Since \(x \in uv\), this forces \(x' \in uv\). \(\square\)

2.3.3 The algorithm

Before stating the algorithm we need one more structural result:

Claim 6 \((\star)\) — Convex subregion guarding
Let \(S \subset P\) be a convex polygon subregion. Then some vertex \(w \in V(P)\) guards \(S\).
Proof. If \(S\) and \(P\) share a vertex we are done. Otherwise, let \(y\) be the closest point of \(\partial P\) to \(S\). If \(y\) does not see all of \(S\), there is a point \(y' \in \partial P\) between \(y\) and an un-seen point, and \(y'\) is strictly closer to \(S\) than \(y\) — contradicting minimality. Hence \(y\) sees \(S\). Sliding \(y\) along \(\partial P\) until the convex hull of \(S \cup \{y\}\) hits a vertex \(w\) of \(P\) gives a vertex that also sees \(S\). \(\square\)
Algorithm 1 — Vertex Guarding a WV-polygon
Input: Polygon \(P\), vertex guard \(G\) guarding \(\partial P\).
Output: \(G' \subseteq V\) such that \(G' \cup G\) guards \(P\).
  1. Compute all constructed edges \(E\) of \(G\).
  2. For each upper edge \(xy \in E\) with \(x \in uv\):
    1. Find the highest intersection point \(p\) of \(xy\) with another constructed edge \(x'y' \in E\), \(x' \in uv\), where \(x'\) is on the same side of \(xy\) as \(C_{xy}\).
    2. If \(p\) exists, find the vertex \(w\) of \(P\) that guards the triangle \(\triangle x p x' \subset P\) (via Claim 6) and add \(w\) to \(G'\).
  3. Return \(G'\).
Claim 7 — Correctness of Algorithm 1
Every hole \(H\) in \(P\) is contained in one of the triangles \(\triangle xpx'\) identified by Algorithm 1.
Proof. By Claims 4 and 5, every hole \(H\) has two successive edges \(h_1 h_2\) and \(h_2 h_3\) on upper edges \(x_{d-1}y_{d-1}\) and \(x_d y_d\) with \(x_{d-1}, x_d \in uv\). Let \(h_2\) be the topmost point of \(H\). If the algorithm finds intersection point \(p\) on \(x_{d-1}y_{d-1}\):
  • If \(p\) is above or equal to \(h_2\), then \(\triangle x_{d-1}p\,x_d\) contains \(H\). ✓
  • If \(p\) is below \(h_1\), then \(x_d y_d\) slopes in the same direction as \(x_{d-1}y_{d-1}\) with smaller absolute slope, contradicting \(h_2\) being the topmost point.
If no \(p\) is found on \(x_{d-1}y_{d-1}\), the same contradiction arises. \(\square\)
Remark 1
Everything above assumes \(u\) and \(v\) are convex vertices. If \(u\) or \(v\) is concave, replace the concave region with the edge \(uw_1\) (resp. \(vw_2\)) to the first vertex that would render \(u\) (resp. \(v\)) convex, and add \(u\) (resp. \(v\)) to the guard set. Any region seen from a vertex in the removed concave area can equally be seen from \(u\) or \(v\) — otherwise weak visibility would be violated.

2.3.4 Approximation factor

Claim 8
Every point \(p \in P\) sees exactly one contiguous sub-segment of \(uv\), and therefore has at most two constructed edges touching \(uv\).
Proof. Let \(a\) and \(b\) be the left- and right-most points of \(uv\) seen by \(p\). Since \(\partial P\) cannot cross \(pa\) or \(pb\), the triangle \(\triangle pab\) contains no boundary point, so \(p\) sees all of \(uv\) between \(a\) and \(b\). \(\square\)
Corollary 8.1
\(p\) has at most one upper edge and one lower edge touching \(uv\).

By Claim 8, Algorithm 1 examines at most \(|G|\) constructed edges (one upper edge per guard), and adds at most one vertex to \(G'\) per guard. Thus \[ |G \cup G'| \;\leq\; 2|G|. \] Since \(|G| \leq (1+\varepsilon)\cdot\OPT\), the total approximation factor is \(2 + 2\varepsilon\), or simply \(2 + \varepsilon\) for a given \(\varepsilon > 0\).

2.3.5 Runtime

  • \(G\) is computed in \(O\!\left(n^{O(\varepsilon^{-2})}\right)\).
  • All constructed edges are computed in \(O(|G|\cdot n) \subseteq O(n^2)\) using Joe & Simpson's algorithm [11][12].
  • At most \(|G|\) edges are examined, so at most \(|G|^2\) intersection candidates; each triangle guard is found in \(O(n)\) via Claim 6.

The theoretical total runtime is \[ O\!\left(n^{\min\!\left(2,\, O(\varepsilon^{-2})\right)}\right). \]

  1. This reasoning only holds when \(u\) and \(v\) are convex; see Remark 1 for the concave case.
