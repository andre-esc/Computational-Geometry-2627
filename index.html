Abstract

We present and analyse an interactive implementation of the constant-factor polynomial-time approximation algorithm of Ashur, Filtser, Katz & Saban (2022) for vertex-guarding a weakly visible polygon (WV-polygon). Starting from Bhattacharya et al.'s boundary guard construction and the terrain-like PTAS of Mustafa & Ray, the algorithm first approximates a minimum boundary guard set for \(\partial P\), then adds a bounded number of additional guards to cover every interior hole, yielding a \((2+\varepsilon)\)-approximation of the global optimum. All algorithms are implemented in TypeScript with live interactive demonstrations throughout.

1 Introduction

The concept of "guarding" a polygon is a highly studied aspect of computational geometry, particularly in association with the Art Gallery problem. To guard a polygon is to place points, called guards, in such a way that every point of the polygon is visible from at least one guard (a point is visible from another point if the line segment connecting them is not obstructed—in the context of polygon guarding, this means that the segment is fully contained within the polygon).

There exist many variants to this problem, which vary the properties of the polygon to be guarded, where we can place guards, or even whether the guards are allowed to move within the polygon. The general Art Gallery problem has been proven to be \(\exists\mathbb{R}\)-complete [1] (meaning that any problem expressed over integer or real number values can be reduced to an instance of the Art Gallery problem). In the case of guarding a simple polygon with guards limited to being placed on polygon vertices, the problem of finding the minimum number of guards (or, more precisely, determining whether a polygon can be guarded with at most \(k\) guards) is NP-hard [2], and even APX-hard [3] (meaning that any NP problem admitting a constant-factor polynomial-time approximation algorithm admits a PTAS that reduces it to this problem).

Subir K. Ghosh presented an \(O(\log n)\)-approximation algorithm for vertex-guarding a simple polygon [4], and conjectured that it must be possible to find a polynomial-time constant-approximation algorithm. Bhattacharya et al. later proposed a 6-approximation algorithm that turned out to be false, as their algorithm guaranteed the polynomial-time guarding of a simple polygon's boundary (up to an approximation), but not its interior [5].

This project aims to demonstrate the constant-factor polynomial-time approximation algorithm discovered by Ashur et al. for vertex-guarding a weakly visible polygon (noted WV-polygons), building upon Bhattacharya et al.'s previous results and confirming Ghosh's conjecture for this subset of simple polygons [6]. This algorithm works by analyzing the geometric properties of a vertex guard of the polygon's boundary, then by adding subsequent guards to cover any unguarded regions within the polygon's interior.

In the following sections, we first discuss how to characterize and detect WV-polygons, then we show how to compute a boundary guard for a WV-polygon, and finally we demonstrate how the algorithm guards the rest of the polygon.

2 Description

2.1 Weakly visible polygons

A polygon is said to be weakly visible from an edge \(u v\) if for all points \(p \in P\), there exists a point \(g \in u v\) that guards (sees) \(p\).

In order to detect whether a given polygon is weakly visible from an edge, we make use of a simple property held by all WV-polygons:

Proposition 1 [7], [8]
Let \(u v\) be an edge in a polygon \(P\). Assume w.l.o.g. that \(u\) is to the right of \(v\). Then, \(P\) is weakly visible from \(u v\) iff.
  • The shortest path from \(u\) to any vertex \(w \in P\) only ever takes left turns, and
  • The shortest path from \(v\) to any vertex \(w \in P\) only ever takes right turns.

To test this property, we calculate what is called the Shortest Path Tree (SPT) of the polygon. Denote \(\pi(u, x)\) the shortest Euclidean path from \(u\) to \(x\) within \(P\). The SPT from vertex \(u\) is then defined as \[\textrm{SPT}_P(u)=\bigcup_{v \in V} \pi(u, v)\]and consists of a tree whose vertices are vertices of \(P\) and whose edges form the shortest paths from \(s\) to all other vertices of \(P\).

To compute \(\textrm{SPT}_P (s)\) from a vertex \(s\) of \(P\), we first triangulate \(P\) and derive the dual graph \(D\) of the triangulation. Then, we iterate through the vertices of \(D\), starting from the triangle containing our root vertex \(s\). As we iterate, we progressively enter and process triangles of the triangulation—each iteration admits a diagonal \(u v\) of the triangulation that we cross (equivalent to the edge of \(D\) that we traverse) and an unprocessed vertex \(w\) of the triangle we just entered.

Let \(a\) be the farthest common ancestor of \(u\) and \(v\) in \(\textrm{SPT}_P (s)\). Then, \(\pi(a, u)\) and \(\pi(a, v)\) form a funnel \(F(u a v)\), i.e. the two paths turn away from each other, and join at \(a\) [8].

We can then compute \(\textrm{SPT}_P (s)\) by constructing funnels as we iterate over \(D\). For each diagonal \(u v\) and corresponding unprocessed vertex \(w\), we get its tangent \(x\) to the funnel \(F(u a v)\). Assume w.l.o.g. that \(x\) lies on \(\pi(a, u)\). Then, we split the funnel into two funnels \(F(u x w)\) and \(F(w a v)\), forming the funnels for adjacent diagonals \(u w\) and \(w v\). We can then add \(x w\) to \(\pi(s, x)\), slowly building \(\textrm{SPT}_P (s)\) as we go along.

Given this algorithm, to test whether a simple polygon \(P\) is weakly visible from edge \(u v\), we

  • Calculate \(\textrm{SPT}_P (u)\) and \(\textrm{SPT}_P (v)\)
  • Verify turn direction in both

2.2 Guarding polygon boundaries

Now that we can feasibly detect weakly visible polygons, the next step is to discuss how we can approximate a minimum vertex guard for the boundary of a polygon \(P\).

To do this, we adapt the work of Ashur et al. [10], specifically their statements pertaining to WV-polygons.

2.2.1 Constructing a witness set

Denote \(\partial P\) as the boundary of polygon \(P\). In order to guard \(\partial P\), we define a witness set \(W\) of points along \(\partial P\) such that, if a set of vertices \(G\) guards \(W\), then \(G\) also guards \(\partial P\).

To do this, we first compute all the visible sections of \(\partial P\) that are visible from each vertex \(v\) of \(P\). The endpoints of all these visible sections split \(\partial P\) into \(O(n^2)\) pieces. If we take an arbitrary point from every piece \(P_i\), we obtain our witness set: if \(G\) guards these points, then \(G\) guards \(\partial P\).

Proof

Assume that \(G\) guards \(W\).

Let \(p_i \in W\) such that \(p_i \in P_i\) and \(g \in G\) such that \(g\) sees \(p_i\). Then, \(p_i\) must be part of a visible section from \(g\), and since \(p_i\) is within \(P_i\), \(g\) must also guard \(P_i\).

Since all \(p_i\) are guarded by a vertex \(g \in G\), the entire boundary \(\partial P\) is guarded. \(\square\)

2.2.2 A PTAS for guarding the witness set

To obtain a PTAS for guarding this witness set, Ashur et al. use the result of Mustafa and Ray [11], which gives an \(O(1+\varepsilon)\)-approximation PTAS for any minimum hitting problem provided that a specific condition is satisfied.

Before proceeding, we must first note two things:

Observation 1

Finding a minimum vertex guard \(G \subset V\) to guard the set of points \(W\) is equivalent to finding the minimum dominating set of the visibility graph of \(G \cup W\).

Indeed, if we find a set of points \(G\) in \(V\) that dominates \(W\) (i.e. every point \(w \in W\) is adjacent to a point in the dominating set \(G\)), this directly implies that all points in \(W\) are visible from at least one point in \(G\), and thus \(W\) is guarded.

Observation 2

The visibility graph of points along the boundary of a WV-polygon are "terrain-like" (as the authors put it), i.e. if, for vertices \(a, b, c, d \in V\) in (counter-)clockwise order, \(a\) sees \(c\) and \(b\) sees \(d\), then \(a\) must see \(d\).

Indeed, if this is not the case, then that means that \(\partial P\) intersects \(a d\) without intersecting \(a c\) or \(b d\). It is easy to see that this would either mean \(a\) is not visible from the base edge \(u v\) (if the intersection happens between \(a\) and \(u\)) or \(d\) is not visible from \(u v\) (if the intersection happens between \(v\) and \(d\)), which is a contradiction of the weak visibility property.

Note: This only holds if \(u\) and \(v\) are convex. See Remark 1 for details.

Therefore, if we find a PTAS for finding the minimum dominating set of a terrain-like graph, we find a PTAS for guarding \(W\) and thus \(\partial P\).

Mustafa and Ray provide a proof scheme for general hitting set problems such as this [11], which, applied to the minimum dominating set problem, admits the following local search algorithm:

Local-search boundary guard
Given a certain precision \(\varepsilon > 0\), set \(k = c^2\varepsilon^{-2}\) with \(c\) a certain constant. Perform the following \(k\)-level local search:
  1. Start with an arbitrary hitting set (in this case, a dominating set \(G \subset V\) of \(W\)).
  2. If any \(k\) points of \(S\) can be replaced with \(k-1\) points of \(V \setminus S\) while still being a hitting set (dominating set of \(W\)), replace and repeat.
  3. Stop when no improving swap exists.

This algorithm returns a dominating set of size at most \(\varepsilon \cdot \textrm{OPT}\), where \(\textrm{OPT}\) is the size of the optimal set.

2.2.3 Proving the PTAS

The condition for this to be a valid PTAS, as stated by Mustafa and Ray, is the locality condition, which states that for any disjoint subsets \(R, B \subseteq \mathcal{P}\) in range space \(R = (\mathcal{P}, \mathcal{D})\) (where \(\mathcal{D}\) contains all subsets of \(\mathcal{P}\)), we can construct a planar bipartite graph \(H\) between \(R\) and \(B\) such that for any range \(D \in \mathcal{D}\), if \(R\) and \(B\) participate in the range (\(R, B \cap \mathcal{D} \neq \emptyset\)) then there exist \(r \in R \cap D\) and \(b \in B \cap D\) such that \((a, b)\) is an edge in \(H\) [11].

For our case, this translates to the following condition: we can construct a planar bipartite graph \(H = (R, B, F)\) between \(R\) and \(B\) such that for every vertex \(v\), there exist \(r in R\) and \(b in B\) such that \(r, b\) dominate \(v\) and \((r,b) \in F\). This is proven by the authors using the terrain-like property of the graph [10], but this is out of scope for the project.

2.2.4 Summary

In short, we can

  1. Compute the witness set of \(P\), then
  2. Apply the approximation algorithm above to find a \((1+\varepsilon)\)-approximation guarding set of \(\partial P\).

Our implementation sets \(k=2\). Assuming that \(\textrm{OPT}\) is greater than \(c^2=8\), we obtain \(\varepsilon=2\), and thus a 3-approximation of the minimum vertex guard (i.e. the obtained result is at most 3 times larger than the minimum).

Additionally, we use a naive implementation, which by Mustafa and Ray, has a running time of \(O(n^{2k+1}) \sim O(n^{O(\varepsilon^{-2})})\) since \(k \sim \varepsilon^{-2}\) up to a constant [10], [11].

2.3 Guarding holes

The above algorithm guarantees the guarding of the boundary \(\partial P\), but does not guarantee the guarding of the entire interior of \(P\). Indeed, guard sets that guard \(\partial P\) but not \(P\) do exist:

In order to ensure that we guard the entirety of \(P\), we must add additional guards \(G'\) that cover any potential "holes" left behind by a boundary guard \(G\). We must analyze the structural properties of said holes, and place additional guards in such a way that we are guaranteed to guard every hole.

First, we will analyze the properties of "constructed edges", then of the holes themselves.

Note: the following sections are inspired by and paraphrased from Ashur et al.'s paper [6]. Any additional comments and interpretations not in the original text, such as the names of the following claims, are marked with a ★.

2.3.1 Properties of constructed edges

A constructed edge formed by a vertex \(v\) is defined as an edge interior to \(P\) formed by the visibility polygon \(\textrm{Vis}(p)\) of \(p\). All constructed edges \(x y\) are adjacent to a corresponding pocket \(C_{x y}\) not part of \(\textrm{Vis}(p)\).

  • \(x y\) is an upper edge if \(C_{x y}\) is below \(x y\), i.e. there exists a point \(p \in x y\) such that all visible points of \(C_{x y}\) from \(p\) are below \(\ell_{x y}\) (where \(\ell_{x y}\) is the line along \(x y\)).
  • \(x y\) is an lower edge if \(C_{x y}\) is above \(x y\), i.e. there exists a point \(p \in x y\) such that all visible points of \(C_{x y}\) from \(p\) are above \(\ell_{x y}\).

Additionally, we denote \(\partial C_{x y}\) the section of \(\partial P\) covered by the pocket \(C_{x y}\).

The following are a few observations for WV-polygons (with \(u\) and \(v\) convex) that will be useful later (assume w.l.o.g. that any constructed edge \(x y\) has \(x\) lower than \(y\)):

Claim 1 — ★ Boundary leaning
Let \(x y\) and \(x'y'\) be two constructed edges such that \(C_{x y}\) and \(y'\) are on the same side of \(x y\). Then, \(y' \in \partial C_{x y}\).
Proof. Let \(w\) be \(x y \cap x'y'\). Since \(y'\) and \(C_{x y}\) are on the same side and \(w \in x y\) sees \(y'\), \(y' \in C_{x y}\). And since \(y' \in \partial P\), that means \(y' \in \partial C_{x y}\). \(\square\)
Claim 2 — ★ Lifted pockets
If \(x \in u v\), \(C_{x y}\) is weakly above \(x\). If not, then \(C_{x y}\) is strictly above \(x\).
Proof.

If \(x \in u v\), then \(C_{x y}\) has to be (weakly) above \(x\) since \(P\) is weakly above \(u v \ni x\) and \(C_{x y} \subset P\).

If not, then \(C_{x y} \cap u v = \emptyset\) since otherwise \(u v \subset C_{x y}\) and \(p\) is not visible from \(u v\).

All points \(z \in C_{x y}\) have a point \(z' \in u v\) that sees \(z\). Since \(z \in C_{x y}\) and \(z \notin C_{x y}\), \(x y\) and \(z z'\) cross. And since \(z'\) is below \(x y\), \(z\) must be above \(x y\), and therefore also above its lower endpoint \(x\). \(\square\)

2.3.2 Properties of holes

Now, we analyze the properties of holes. A hole \(H\) in \(P\) with regards to guard \(G\) is a maximal convex connected subregion of \(P\) such that no point in \(H\) is visible from \(G\).

We can make the following evident observations:

Observation 3

All holes are convex, since they are formed by the intersection of halfplanes defined by constructed edges (one side is visible, the other is not; holes are the intersections of non-visible regions).

Observation 4

Every hole touches at least one upper edge and one lower edge.

We will now show that every hole \(H\) is attached to two intersecting constructed edges \(x y\) and \(x'y'\) with endpoints \(x, x' \in u v\), then use this fact to find guards for all \(H\) in polynomial time.

Claim 3 — ★ Downward hole boundary curving
Let \(H=(h_1,\dots,h_k)\). If \(h_1 h_2\) is along an upper edge \(x y\) such that \(x\) is below \(y\) and \(x \notin u v\), then \(h_2 h_3\) is along another upper edge \(x'y'\) with \(h_2\) lower than \(h_3\).
Proof.

Since \(x \notin u v\), \(H \subset C_{x y}\) is strictly above \(x\) (by Claim 2).

Assume that \(y'\) is on the same side of \(x y\) as \(h_3\), and therefore \(C_{x y}\). Then by Claim 1, \(y' \in \partial C_{x y}\), and by Claim 2, \(y' \in C_{x y}\) is strictly above \(x\) (since \(x \notin u v\)).

Additionally, \(x \in \partial C_{x'y'}\) by the same reasoning.

Now, if \(x'\) is above \(y'\) (and thus \(h_2\) is above \(h_3\)), \(y'\) is the lower endpoint and thus \(x \in C_{x'y'}\) is (strictly or weakly) above \(y'\). However, we know that \(x\) is strictly below \(y' \in C_{x y}\), which is a contradiction.

Therefore, \(y'\) is above \(x'\), and \(h_2\) is lower than \(h_3\). \(\square\)

Informally, this means that as long as the upper edge associated with a hole edge doesn't touch \(u v\), the boundary of the hole cannot start curving downwards.

This is formalized in the following claim:

Claim 4 — ★ \(uv\)-touching
Any hole \(H = (h_1,\dots,h_k)\) touches an upper edge with an endpoint in \(u v\).
Proof.

We know that \(H\) touches at least one upper edge. Assume w.l.o.g. that \(h_1 h_2\) is along upper edge \(x_1 y_1\). If \(x_1 y_1\) does not touch \(u v\), then by Claim 3, \(h_2 h_3\) is also along an upper edge \(x_2 y_2\) with \(h_2\) below \(h_3\), and so on.

Since \(H\) is convex, we know that at some point, \(H\) has an edge \(h_i h_{i+1}\) along an upper edge such that \(h_i\) is above \(h_{i+1}\), and therefore at some point, \(H\) has an edge along an upper edge \(x_j y_j\) that touches \(u v\) (to allow downward curving, by Claim 3). \(\square\)

Additionally, it is easy to prove then that subsequent upper edges also touch \(u v\):
Claim 5 — ★ Subsequent \(uv\)-touching
If \(h_1 h_2\) is along an upper edge \(x y\) such that \(x\) is below \(y\) and \(x \in u v\), then \(h_2 h_3\) is along an upper edge \(x'y'\) also such that \(x'\) is below \(y'\) and \(x' in u v\).
Proof. Since \(x y\) and \(x'y'\) intersect at \(h_2\), and \(h_1 \in C_{x'y'}\) is on the same side of \(x' y'\) as \(x\), \(x \in \partial C_{x'y'}\) (by Claim 1) and thus \(x\) is (strictly or weakly) above \(x'\) (by Claim 2). However, \(x \in u v\), so the only way for \(x\) to be strictly or weakly above \(x'\) is for \(x' \in u v\) to also be true. \(\square\)

★ For illustrative purposes (unnecessary for logical proof but helpful to visualize for later), let us define a "downward curving" point as follows: \(h_2\) is a downward curving point if \(h_1 h_2\) and \(h_2 h_3\) are along upper edges, and the slope of \(h_1 h_2\) and the slope of \(h_2 h_3\) differ in sign.

Now, note that even in the case of downward curving, the "downward" constructed edge \(x_d y_d\) must still touch \(u v\) since otherwise, by similar reasoning to Claim 2, \(u v \subset C_{x_d y_d}\) and \(p\) is not visible from \(u v\).

2.3.3 The algorithm

Now that we have proven that every hole \(H\) touches at least two upper edges \(x y\) and \(x'y'\) with \(x, x' \in u v\), we can now tackle the main algorithm proposed by the paper [6].

First, we show the following:

Claim 6 — ★ Convex subregion guarding
Let \(S\) be a simple convex polygon subregion of \(P\) (\(S \subset P\)). Then, there exists a vertex \(v\) of \(P\) that guards \(S\).
Proof.

If \(S\) and \(P\) share a vertex, then that vertex guards \(S\). If not, we have the following reasoning.

First, we prove that there is a point \(y \in \partial P\) that guards \(S\):

Let \(y\) be the point on \(\partial P\) closest to \(S\). If \(y\) does not guard \(S\), then \(y \notin \partial S\) and there exists a point \(t \in S\) that is not visible from \(y\), i.e. there exists a point \(y' \in \partial P\) between \(y\) and \(t\).

However, it is easy to see that \(y'\) would be closer to \(S\) than \(y\): assume w.l.o.g. that the edge of \(S\) closest to \(y'\) is horizontal and that \(y'\) is above \(S\). Then, since \(t \in S\) and \(y'\) is above \(S\), \(y\) is above \(y'\). This means that \(y'\) must be closer to \(S\) than \(y\), a contradiction.

Then, we find a vertex guarding \(S\):

We can slide \(y\) along \(\partial P\) in any direction until the convex hull of \(S \cup y\) hits a vertex \(w\) of \(P\).

\(w\) is then either an endpoint reached by \(y\), or another vertex of \(P\) along the tangent of \(S\) to \(y\). Either way, it is easy to see that \(w\) guards \(S\). \(\square\)

Proving this gives us the ability to formulate the following algorithm:

Algorithm 1 — Vertex Guarding a WV-polygon
Input: Polygon \(P\), vertex guard \(G\) guarding \(\partial P\).
Output: \(G' \subset V\) such that \(G' \cup G\) guards \(P\).
  1. Compute all constructed edges \(E\) of \(G\).
  2. For each upper edge \(xy \in E\) with \(x \in uv\):
    1. Find the highest intersection point \(p\) of \(xy\) with another constructed edge \(x'y' \in E\), \(x' \in uv\) such that \(x'\) is on the same side of \(x y\) as \(C_{x y}\).
    2. If \(p\) exists, find the vertex \(w\) of \(P\) that guards the triangle \(\triangle x p x' \subset P\) (possible via Claim 6) and add it to \(G'\).
  3. Return \(G'\).
Claim 7
The above algorithm is correct, i.e. any hole \(H\) in \(P\) is contained in one of the triangles covered by the algorithm.
Proof.

By Claims 4 and 5, we know that every hole \(H\) has two edges \(h_1 h_2\) and \(h_2 h_3\) touching two upper edges \(x_{d-1} y_{d-1}\) and \(x_d y_d\) with \(x_{d-1}, x_d \in u v\). Let these edges be the crossing point of the slope along \(H\), i.e. \(h_1 h_2\) is the edge of \(H\) with minimum absolute slope, or \((star)\) \(h_2\) is the downward curving point. Note then that \(h_2\) is the topmost point of \(H\).

If the algorithm found an intersection point \(p\) on \(x_{d-1} y_{d-1}\), then either:

\(p\) is above (or equal to) \(h_2\), in which case \(triangle x_{d-1} p x_d\) definitely contains \(H\), or

\(p\) is below (or equal to) \(h_1\), in which case \(x_d y_d\) has the same slope direction as \(x_{d-1} y_{d-1}\) with smaller absolute slope (★ i.e. \(h_2\) is not the downward curving point), a contradiction.

If the algorithm did not find \(p\) on \(x_{d-1} y_{d-1}\), then since \(p\) is the highest intersection point, ★ \(h_2\) once again cannot be the downward curving point, a contradiction.

Remark 1
Everything above assumes \(u\) and \(v\) are convex vertices. If \(u\) or \(v\) is concave, replace the concave region with the edge \(uw_1\) (resp. \(vw_2\)) to the first vertex that would render \(u\) (resp. \(v\)) convex, and add \(u\) (resp. \(v\)) to the guard set. Any region seen from a vertex in the removed concave area can equally be seen from \(u\) or \(v\) — otherwise weak visibility would be violated.

2.3.4 Approximation factor

Claim 8
Every point \(p \in P\) sees exactly one contiguous sub-segment of \(uv\), and therefore has at most two constructed edges touching \(uv\).
Proof. Let \(a\) and \(b\) be the left- and right-most points of \(uv\) seen by \(p\). Since \(\partial P\) cannot cross \(pa\) or \(pb\), the triangle \(\triangle pab\) contains no boundary point, so \(p\) sees all of \(uv\) between \(a\) and \(b\). \(\square\)
Corollary 8.1
\(p\) has at most one upper edge and one lower edge touching \(uv\).

By Claim 8, Algorithm 1 examines at most \(|G|\) constructed edges (one upper edge per guard), and adds at most one vertex to \(G'\) per guard. Thus \[ |G \cup G'| \;\leq\; 2|G|. \] Since \(|G| \leq (1+\varepsilon)\cdot\OPT\), the total approximation factor is \(2 + 2\varepsilon\), or simply \(2 + \varepsilon\) for a given \(\varepsilon > 0\).

2.3.5 Runtime

  • \(G\) is computed in \(O\!\left(n^{O(\varepsilon^{-2})}\right)\).
  • All constructed edges are computed in \(O(|G|\cdot n) \subseteq O(n^2)\) using Joe & Simpson's algorithm [11][12].
  • At most \(|G|\) edges are examined, so at most \(|G|^2\) intersection candidates; each triangle guard is found in \(O(n)\) via Claim 6.

The theoretical total runtime is \[ O\!\left(n^{\min\!\left(2,\, O(\varepsilon^{-2})\right)}\right). \]

  1. This reasoning only holds when \(u\) and \(v\) are convex; see Remark 1 for the concave case.
